The Shards · Bret Easton Ellis · 2023 · memory, desire, and violence in Los Angeles

The Shards begins as recollection and slowly reveals recollection as a trap. An older Bret Easton Ellis looks back at his final year at Buckley in 1981, when a new student entered his circle and a serial killer called the Trawler moved through Los Angeles. The past arrives with suspicious precision.

Ellis gives the world the hard surface of autobiography: streets, songs, restaurants, cars, houses, names. Detail creates authority. It also creates camouflage. The more exact the setting becomes, the easier it is to accept the narrator's fear as fact and his desire as somebody else's guilt.

The novel is long because obsession is repetitive. Bret watches, circles, imagines, returns. Information does not resolve the mystery; it feeds it. The same gesture can look like evidence, jealousy, or fantasy depending on which version of Bret is speaking — the teenager inside the event or the author arranging it decades later.

Privilege gives the characters freedom without safety. Adults are absent, money removes ordinary consequences, and Los Angeles becomes a sealed system of pools, bedrooms, private schools, and empty roads. Everything is exposed. Nobody is available to see it clearly.

The violence matters less as a puzzle than as pressure on the voice telling it. Ellis keeps asking whether the danger entered Bret's life from outside, or whether Bret needed an outside danger to explain what he could not admit about himself. The Trawler is a killer. He is also a structure the narrator can hide inside.

The Shards is one of Ellis's strongest books because its excess has a purpose. It does not simply reconstruct youth. It shows memory editing youth into a story the adult can survive. By the end, the mystery is not what happened. It is who has been controlling the light.

Rating: major
