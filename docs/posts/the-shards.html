>> the shards

FX · 2026 · Ryan Murphy, Bret Easton Ellis · Igby Rigney, Kaia Gerber, Homer Gere

The central problem in adapting The Shards is not the serial killer. It is the narrator. Bret remembers Los Angeles in 1981 with impossible detail, then quietly makes every detail suspect. The novel works because memory is both the evidence and the thing contaminating it.

Television has to make that uncertainty visible. Igby Rigney's Bret watches his friends, edits himself around them, and turns the arrival of Robert Mallory into a private investigation. Robert may be dangerous. Bret may be obsessed. The distinction keeps changing according to who controls the scene.

Ryan Murphy is an obvious fit for the surfaces: expensive houses, controlled bodies, music, cars, and the hard light of Los Angeles. Ellis is interested in what those surfaces conceal. The series is strongest when the beauty feels less like pleasure than an alibi everyone has agreed to maintain.

The Trawler gives the story an external threat, but the murders are not its only source of dread. The deeper tension is social. These teenagers know how to look composed before they know how to understand desire, jealousy, or fear. Privilege protects them from consequences while leaving them almost defenceless against themselves.

The adaptation does not need to solve Bret. It needs to preserve the possibility that he is telling the truth and arranging it at the same time. So far, it holds that line. The mystery matters. The person remembering it matters more.
